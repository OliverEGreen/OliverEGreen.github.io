# Introduction

Let's be fair.

The ferocious speed and scale of *big-picture* change that we're currently living through can be terrifying. Frankly, it probably *should* be—like the [kneecap test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patellar_reflex) that proves we're still functioning normally.

As we barrel towards the fever dream of AI being better than us at almost everything[^1], our organic human abilities begin to look very outclassed, glacial and meagre. Furthermore, the sheer magnitude of the financial support backing this change can make the endgame feel inevitable. It's a lot to take in.

[^1]: [Anything you can do, I can do better.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WO23WBji_Z0)

Most importantly: what becomes of us?

In this post, I'd like to offer some thoughts toward one of the big existential questions I see people wrestling with: *"what will we do with ourselves now?"*

Amid the din of hype, visionary proclamations and controversy, I've not seen much in the way of practical, everyday advice on how to address our role in a world that no longer strictly requires us.

**And I believe this is really important.**

A lot of people will feel rudderless when they don't have a direction to exert their efforts towards. Maybe *forty-something* extra hours per week. That's a lot of life to suddenly have on your hands.

What would we do with it?

Honestly, probably a lot more sleeping. Scientists are increasingly saying that's good for you anyway, so there's an easy win. But, beyond that, it can feel like a gigantic, nebulous open question with no great answers.

My goal with this piece is to (hopefully) inspire you, and remind you that we haven't completed the game of life *just yet*. In fact, the good news is that there's an embarrassment of side-quests left for us to finally engage with, now that we have the time. But we could benefit from some new mental tooling to make the most of this transition.

## Hosing ourselves down

How we frame this situation matters; it's commonly observed how my exceedingly-corporatised generation (Millennials) has an unusually hard time separating their self-worth from their professional achievements[^2]. If this is true, it's a lens we've been seeing our lives through and one we'll need to learn how to remove.

[^2]: McKinsey & Co [explicitly](https://anant.us/blog/team-blog/inspire/fortune-how-mckinsey-does-it/) [state](https://www.ft.com/content/ba0c9234-a2d7-11e7-9e4f-7f5e6a7c98a2) [that](https://www.efinancialcareers.com/news/2013/06/goldman-sachs-lures-insecure-overachievers-says-ex-goldman-trader) they seek ['insecure overachievers'](https://www.forefrontmag.com/2015/01/leading-the-insecure-overachiever/) when hiring. People whose self-worth is explicitly tied to their ability to thrive in the corporate grind.

How did we become this way?

As Millennials in the West, we grew up during an unusually calm and stable societal period. By the time we reached young adulthood, the path to success, wealth and glory was well-established; ours to take should we choose.

The grand-picture struggles of previous generations (e.g. civil rights) didn't really apply to us; we knew of them as historical events, sure. We were thankful that they happened. But our reality was that we grew up surrounded, coddled, by *almost-legendary* institutions, rich with historic significance. Unlike prior generations, we didn't mind this situation and so we never really learned to rebel.

For ourselves, the game plan was logical:

* Work hard, get good grades, be admitted into and graduate from a prestigious university. 
* Find your way into one of these elite institutions and slowly grind your way up the ladder. All of the good things would surely follow.

It seemed like a good deal. But along with this almost pre-packaged life path, we slowly learned to bend our will to that of the institutions, becoming, ourselves, institutionalised.

You don't need to work a job in high finance or law to have felt the impact of what I'm describing. It's more subtly pervasive than that—a shift in the Zeitgeist itself, the way we understand, act upon and gauge the value of things.

Our workplaces, and even our personal lives began to bend towards the immense black hole gravity of the institution. With this, we began self-policing the thoughts and opinions we'd share during times of real moral crisis.

We'd silently poll ourselves before speaking up: "how could this impact my job?". We've all heard about people being cancelled for posting the wrong thing. And high power often expects us to stay on-brand and on-message at all times.

Another place our commercialised nature most clearly surfaces is during our leisure time. The instinct sits in the back of one's head, slowly sapping away any joy or passion from our hobbies.

I've witnessed friends in London follow new interests, investing their time and energy before eventually—almost reflexively—confronting the familiar creeping thought... "*how can I make money from this? Is there a business here I can grow?*"

Who could blame them. Under big-city competitive pressures, the idea of spending our precious leftover time on a leisurely pursuit for the sheer joy of it can seem audacious, even irrational, like moving against the herd:

* Should I take on a new sport? Will it make me hotter? Will it help me find a suitable partner?
* Shall I start drawing or painting? Can I begin exhibiting and selling prints so I can afford a larger mortgage? Maybe even a second home?

Our generation became very value-literate, transactional and end goal focussed. We mastered *deferred gratification*. We know all of the personal finance 'hacks', learned about risk-aware stock trading strategies and achievable tax loopholes. To some degree—and despite knowing better—we became quite superficial and materialistic. 

Sometimes, we'd be rewarded for our efforts with a new job title, a podcast interview or even winning some kind of prize. Perhaps we'd be featured as a successful alumnus in our university magazine, or host a panel discussion full of renowned industry talking heads.

As this progressed, the once-sulfurous smell of a corporate rat race job became normalised, even aspirational. Sure, Patrick Bateman was a bad guy... but at least he could afford the monthly mortgage payments, right?

Lifestyle anxiety can be a darkly-convincing force for those lying awake in the middle of the night, slowly drowning beneath serious financial pressures.

Personally, I know four architects who moved into fintech; in any healthy society this should surely never be possible. In many fields, MBAs became almost obligatory as the price of entry to being taken seriously.

It's worth clarifying that I have nothing against people's ambitions or in striving for a better life. My axe to grind is that the central equation underlying these life strategies <u>doesn't seem to be adding up any more</u>. Overproduction of the elites is a well-documented phenomenon, and a timebomb our generation has yet to reckon with.

Our top universities diluted their good names, transformed into investment vehicles and started flooding the global cities with a deluge of new graduates. Almost overnight, they transformed into a hive full of quietly desperate queen bees. Each having rightfully earned their throne. Each having put in the hard work, strategic research and developed an almost obsequious level of obedience to the institutions which, in turn, would elevate our toil.

In parallel, these institutions—the very bones of our culture—began to atrophy and visibly rot before our eyes. Between the endless leaks, the scandals and exposés, public trust in media and political institutions is at an all-time low. In the UK, our beloved BBC has been exposed as a bustling haven of paedophilia, stretching back for decades. Even our royal family can't escape the tarnish. 

Where did our good work leave us?

In serving the machine, we slowly became more machine-shaped. We pinched our noses and signed the Faustian bargain. It was either that, or see the dreams of our youth (home ownership, exotic holidays, financial freedom) accelerate away from us. Hell, most of us would settle just to have what our parents had.

Again—I'm not here to judge (I really mean that!).

This is a perfectly rational response to the set of circumstances we were given. It certainly beats becoming a NEET or a wastrel. We were young once and had time and energy to chuck towards something anyway. And the perks and treats were sometimes fairly nice.

To varying degrees, we became comfortable with living to work; an imbalanced and imperfect relationship, sure, but it was still better than nothing.

And then work left us.

## Working to live

Ok. This hasn't happened *just yet*, of course—but we're plenty able to read the room.

Like any relationship that becomes imbalanced, it will carry on through momentum for a while before the inevitable happens. Our worklessness is a scenario we need to plan for, or at least consider.

At least, here there is some good news.

After a quick dip into history, it seems that life hasn't always been about the endless, awful grindset[^3]. Oddly, our current moment might be less daunting to someone from the 1700s.

Back then, the average person would have seen life through a fundamentally different lens to us: believing that their place in the universe was divinely granted, never needing to be justified through hard work or achievement.

[^3]: The Atlantic: [How life became an endless, terrible competition](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2019/09/meritocracys-miserable-winners/594760/).

Productivity as a virtue within itself was something that arose organically through the course of the Industrial Revolution and—with it—came those familiar dreams of entrepreneurial success, wealth and status. Those antiquated ideas still haunt us today.

A few years ago, in search of alternate life lenses, I read Bertrand Russell's *In Praise of Idleness*. I researched the Luddites and the *dark Satanic mills*[^4] they rallied against. I wanted to get a sense of the life that these people (correctly) feared could be lost; my instinct was that I could find some useful starting points there.

[^4]: William Blake's [Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/And_did_those_feet_in_ancient_time).

My reading is that these people enjoyed a slower pace of life, one which we'd probably describe as 'wholesome'. People's on-paper lifestyles mattered less; quietly living well every day mattered much more. Social media posts and personal branding seminars were a long-distant dream.

These people paid more attention to their communities, the values they collectively upheld, and nurtured their relations towards each other. They engaged with and reinforced their local traditions and practised religion, with family being the most important social unit of all. Big cities granted us anonymity in exchange for alienation, but at our cores we fundamentally remain social, simian creatures. 

People of the past focussed more on local problems within their grasp, instead of hyperfocussing on random new atrocities springing up anywhere on the planet. The 24-hour news cycle made us aware, but didn't make us any happier—it just kept us anxious, miserable and addicted to bad news (conveniently so, if your business happens to be ad-supported).

Finally, these people observed nature and the passing of the seasons more closely. They were able to name the native plants, trees and species around them. They knew which mushrooms were edible, or which plants could make for useful remedies or ingredients.

This is all quaint and Oldey-Worldey, I know. What I'm selling you here is very much old, reheated goods.

The timeless answers to the timeless questions are nothing especially fresh or original. Nevertheless we've done our level-best to discard every one of them throughout the 20th century. Maybe it was hype, maybe hubris. And now we're searching through the scrap heap to try and find remnants of the comfort and stability we grew up surrounded by. The 1990s feel a long way away these days.

These timeless things begin to matter a lot more once our terminal-stage careers decide to up sticks and disappear forever. And I believe they contain part of the answer to our burning question ("what shall we do with ourselves?").

There's another shift in framing coming.

## Ending soft slavery

Our species has very rarely been without some form of toil. We've always had, to some degree, a love-hate relationship to our labours.

Work greedily helps itself to our waking hours, to our time and mental energy. Sometimes it feels like we're forced to live our lives in the precious slivers of time between clocking off and sleeping.

But for many people, it also defines us. *What we do* is one of the first things we talk about as we get to know someone. Our job can provide a part of our identity or broadcast our social value within a community. This can have its upsides. 

But, ultimately, our work *has been obligatory*. 

Almost everyone is implicitly forced to work in order to provide themselves with food, shelter and accommodate their everyday needs. Under this implied duress, it is possible to make the argument that this arrangement amounts to a form of *soft slavery*.

Perhaps, here's where that obligatory aspect starts to change. And if we choose to work, we do so with total freedom.

I'd like to introduce yet another unoriginal idea; using our spare time to pursue something that truly interests us, with the commercial pressures off, and as a purely joyful, playful and creative exercise. We'll put our profiteering mindset off to the side.

## Geeking out for the sheer bloody hell of it

Let's briefly look back in time again.

The Industrial Revolution brought about an unusual concentration of wealth and power, from which emerged the phenomenon of the middle class. No longer were we purely serfs and aristocrats; a small number of people began to occupy this novel middle ground.

Given the Victorian social hierarchy, much of this spare time and wealth wound up congregating in the hands of the clergy. In this context, something unusual happened; a scholarly class began to develop.

This is significant because people often seem to unconsciously follow a fairly standard playbook when we acquire substantial wealth or power:

* First, we gather a harem.
* Then, we abuse drugs.
* Finally, we declare war on our enemies and generally make foolish 'fuck-you money' type decisions.

We've done this since time immemorial; I guess it's our path of least resistance. From Genghis Khan through to the Tates.

However, this clergy class had an unusually strong moral backbone and work ethic (they were Victorians, after all). They dedicated much of their spare time to scholarly pursuits: reading, engaging in correspondence and conducting experiments.

They tinkered and they toyed, and they recorded. From this, we entered a Victorian age of intense curiosity and scientific discovery, seeing fundamental leaps forward in our understanding of biology, physics and mathematics[^6].

[^6]: Mendel et al.

We owe them a great debt. They did this all for *love of the game*. Many of the aforementioned glorious institutions were founded during this period.

Of course, such periods of discovery have happened before. Just think of the ancient Greek scholars, poets, philosophers, the famous Arabian mathematicians or technology of ancient China. 

Sure. *Good for them*, I guess...?

Ok, yes, it can sometimes feel like everything's already been discovered. The old boys with the impressive-sounding surnames figured it all out before we were born, right?

Fortunately for us, this is merely an illusion. We actually have *so much* yet to discover. Instead, I'd argue the reality is that our overly left-brained corporatised lives have caused us to forget how to be curious or creative.

But you need not look far to see through the illusion; one example is we're currently living through a golden age of curious, competent, hungry and well-funded content creators.[^7]

[^7]: NileRed, Backyard Scientist, Veritasium, 3Blue1Brown.

Any cursory reading of their channels will show that they're genuinely onto something good and almost all of these creators began more or less from scratch. Watch Clickspring [machine the Antikythera Mechanism](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ML4tw_UzqZE) using period-correct tools, [Primitive Technology](https://www.youtube.com/@primitivetechnology) building civilisation from mud in wordless silence, or Tasting History [recreating the carbonised bread of Pompeii](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sw2qrt6tOKw). Meanwhile, the [Vesuvius Challenge](https://scrollprize.org/) is quietly using AI to read the ancient scrolls that Vesuvius carbonised—without ever unrolling them. And I think it's only a matter of time before a YouTuber will uncover some genuinely new area of science. It's just one example, but it shows that we can learn to get curious again—it's not even that hard.

I believe unlocking our curiosity and embracing our inner geekery forms the second part of our answer.

We've covered a lot already, so here's a brief recap:

* I'm arguing for us living a slower, more wholesome and local existence. 
* I want you to embrace some form of geekery for sheer *love of the game*.

And just to keep you on your toes, I'll address the second point first.

# Part One: An Age of Discovery

I suspect we're long-overdue another new age of discovery.

We'll soon have all the necessary ingredients gathered together: spare time, energy and access to the latest research in our fields (whatever that may be). All that's needed now is the spark of passion to set the whole thing off.

Two things give me hope in this regard:

* Humans naturally tend to be better at the bleeding edge of our knowledge than machines: Large Language Models are largely introspection machines, unsuitable for truly pioneering extrapolatory tasks.
* We're seeing content creators emerge from all around the world with breathtaking levels of expertise and lofty ambitions[^9] for their pioneering projects.

[^9]: [HTX Studio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XhUuhl9iWpQ).

To this end, I've tried to assimilate as many loose threads, directions and potential sources of inspiration as I can muster.

## Discovering the scientific fundamentals

Directions in which we can still make in-roads towards our understanding of our world, our universe and the deep fundamentals which seem to subtly permeate everything. It's been a surprisingly long time since we discovered any genuinely groundbreaking science. Beyond padding in gaps at the edges of our models and theories[^8], our generation has been notably lightweight in this regard. Hawking was probably our last great scientist who changed the way we see the world.

[^8]: Higgs Boson, gravitational waves.

*Please note, I'm intentionally ignoring technology—the application of science. We've lived through an absolute deluge of new and impressive technologies.*

Below, is an incomplete list of open fields. Some of these you can engage with as a personal hobbyist, while others would require a team of thousands. Either way, I've lumped them all in together. 

### Uncovering a unified model of physics

The Unified Model is one of the oldest Holy Grails in all of science. Despite centuries of effort, nobody has ever managed to successfully unify Newton's physical laws with those that rule quantum mechanics.

And yet, we'd surely like to think that both of these are just subsets of one grander suite of laws that determines everything, regardless of scale.  

### Space stuff

While deep space exploration remains the sole preserve of ultrabillionaires and national governments, there's still a surprising amount you can enjoy from even your back garden.

Our understanding of space is still incredibly young. It was only a few hundred years ago that we fully embraced heliocentrism. Pluto was a planet only yesterday.

Astrophysics and astronomy. The large array in the Chilean desert. We're still building powerful new telescopes on the ground. We also sent up the JWST which is already changing our understanding of outer space.

Astrophotography. This one is surprisingly accessible to anyone with a lot of time and patience. You'll require access to an area without too much light pollution, and probably a semi-decent camera. There's a thriving community on Reddit that is able to capture incredible phenomena from their back gardens. Go outside and spot the ISS while we still have it! Bonus side quest: photographing rare atmospheric phenomena such as sprites.

Astrobiology, helping us understand the origins of life, finding amino acid chains on foreign planets and asteroids, seeing whether life was perhaps seeded on Earth by a foreign object.

Learning how to recognise the constellations and navigate using sextants. 

### Material science

Leaps forward in material science can cause a chain reaction; enabling us to build better technologies with which to carry out our experiments and plough forward into the unknown reaches of physics, chemistry, biology and so on.

One huge potential excitement from a few years ago was the (sadly, false) alarm that university researchers had discovered a room-temperature superconductor material, called LK-99. This is the kind of discovery which, if replicable, could potentially pave the way for absolutely sci-fi level technology. 

Another area of research that's seen a lot of current interest is in manufacturing artificial spider silk[^10]. Expensive and time-consuming to extract from live spiders, this material has an unmatched strength-to-lightness ratio which, again, if harnessed properly could find its way into our skyscrapers, bridges or beyond.

[^10]: Veritasium video on spider silk

### Biology and biotech

What was once joked about as the 'unsexy science' has become belle of the ball in recent decades. We've seen an incredible leap forward, whether CRISPR-Cas9 or Google Deepmind using AI to solve the protein folding prediction problem once and for all.

New therapies for old diseases are dropping like hotcakes.

It's hard to know what this will mean for us; what does life look like when we have infinite longevity and no disease? And beyond merely curing disease, could we be able to extend our abilities and grant ourselves superpowers? Maybe one day we'll all be tetrachromats, able to see new colours.

### Mathematics

It's been a strange few years for the field of mathematics. Back in May, OpenAI's latest model was able to disprove a long-established open conjecture; a new milestone for AI intelligence, just as significant as DeepBlue's victory over Garry Kasparov. 

We're in August now and new mathematical proofs are being published practically every single day. OpenAI just released 10 of them in a single batch.

But maths isn't just a solvable field; it's also a vast open area for exploration and play. There is no better demonstration of this than 3Blue1Brown's recent interview with Dwarkesh Patel.

## Discovering our world

### The oceans

Our planet is overwhelmingly covered in water, and yet our largest expanse is still a mystery to us. We don't even have to leave earth—we only need sufficient curiosity, money and willpower to start exploring the oceans. That is remarkably close-by for a genuine frontier.

I've been enjoying watching Gabe Newell (yes—the founder of Steam) [building out his $815m research fleet](https://robbreport.com/motors/marine/gabe-newell-vard-research-vessel-1238270958/) to explore them. You can follow along on YouTube. I'd like to see others with similar means get in on the action as well.

We barely even have decent maps of the sea floor. The lower we seem to dive into the oceans, the more extreme the life becomes. Creatures that exist in underwater volcanoes. Jellyfish species that resemble a mobile disco.

### Glacier ice

The earth's freezer is continent-sized and full of historical riches. Arctic and Antarctic drill samples act like time capsules, giving us a rich understanding of a wide slice of time that no other medium possibly can.

Furthermore, historic ice ages mixed with our contemporary deep glacial melts mean that artifacts, plants and bodies (human or animal) that have been frozen in time for thousands of years are resurfacing—before quickly degrading. We have a rare opportunity to capture and study these specimens before they are lost forever.

### Geological history

The planet has its own history and it's something we can learn about. 

Recently, a controversial geological theory has emerged regarding an impact during the Younger Dryas period. There seems to be decent geological evidence for this in soil samples; we can trace historic fires (such as the Great Fire of London), volcanic eruptions and asteroid impacts through layers in the dirt. 

Besides this, what other grand geological events could we uncover evidence for?

### The deepest forests

There are still a few remote forests and jungles left on this Earth where animals have no memory of ever seeing a human. Remarkably, they're often not afraid so much as curious about us.

## Discovering Ourselves

### Paleoanthropology

Our ancestry. How we moved around the planet. How our sub-species intermingled. Evolutionary dead ends.

The Peruvian mummies?

### Archeology

It's everywhere. Right under our feet are countless beautiful artifacts that can tell us stories of different lives and worldviews. LIDAR scanning unlocking new insights. Hidden ancient tombs scattering the earth. Billions of dead came before us. What about all the sunken, preserved boats in the Caspian sea? The Roman emperors' enormous boats?

Was Atlantis real? Evidence of a giant Mediterranean flood, you can still go see it on Google Maps today!

Have we ever carried out serious archeological digs in Doggerland, the hidden ancient country just off British shores?

### Our stories

How about our archives? Constantly hearing of lost works, letters, etc being found in museum archives. They have way more than they can handle. Couldn't we digitise them all, put them online and have AI find the patterns, make the connections? Create an interconnected web of ancient knowledge.

### Our minds

Our internal worlds - We hardly know ourselves, let alone others. Perhaps there's now the time for real healing and therapy to happen.

Discovering our minds. Probably a level of psychedelic exploration as well.

## Exploring the biggest mysteries

For those with the appetite, we can put the hard-STEM to the side for the moment and consider the larger mysteries of our existence.

I've often had the unsatisfying sense that several of our closely-held ideas seem to be *explaining away* a mystery, rather than reaching the core of the phenomenon in question. Sometimes it feels like society's best rationalisations don't fully add up to what we experience.

I've collected these together in a separate article—a long list of threads to pull upon for the curious-minded: [Mysteries & Magics](https://olliegreen.info/posts/2/mysteries-and-magics/)

# Part Two: An Age of Recovery

Beyond discovery, there are problems in our midst that we already know the solutions to. What I'd like to see is something of an age of recovery.

The 20th century was a wild ride for Western culture. Our species has a nasty habit of burning our fingers on every possible hot stove before course-correcting; we overdo things, we go to extremes. Common sense often does prevail, eventually, but it's rarely been our first approach.

## Reversing population decline

How we relate to one another. For us to **learn how to communicate again**, if not better than ever before. A telepathy at the feeling and soul or energy level.

Having children will probably come back into fashion. Perhaps the birth rate returns to normal. It gives us purpose, meaning, an identity, and we don't want robots to raise our children. It's a beautiful, profound thing and it's automation-proof!

## Reversing pollution

### Re-wilding projects.

Beavers, wolves, buffalo.

Native plant/tree species.

Conservation - seed banking. 

Trees - Britain is so devoid of actual forestry. Incentives to re-wild parts of the UK.

### De-extinction projects

Colossal biosciences. Absolutely sci-fi level stuff. Attempting to de-extinct the woolly mammoth. They've already created woolly mice. And de-extincted direwolves (yes from Game of Thrones).

### Tackling pollution

Microplastics and PFAS.

## Reversing societal decline

Perhaps a rebuilding of society—of the norms that suit us as a whole. Respect, a return of public behavioural norms. Restoring trust in authority.

Maybe letters will come back. Real proper ones. Not generated content but that slow, considered and highly personal hobby of communicating with loved ones. Men and women of letters.

# Conclusion

I hope I've given you many reasons to have faith that things may yet work out. 

Broadly speaking, I remain hopeful and optimistic for the world we're stepping into. We all have access to an unprecedented amount of information. People are understandably highly anxious right now—you can sense it as you speak with them. But we can also be heartened that so many of us are paying attention. 

Humanity is largely *not too bad* at self-correction in the long term. To me at least, despite everything, it still feels like we're heading squarely towards the good ending. We can't take it for granted, but I think that's something worth taking to heart.

Even if you're currently in full-time employment (as no doubt you are) it's probably worth sketching out your escape route now. With any luck, a couple of the loose threads above might sound like a promising direction, something worth exploring.

We only have to fall in love with *doing* and *being* and *becoming* again. There's all this reality left to explore and encounter. I've said it many times before, but it's worth reiterating: 

> AI can never climb a mountain or swim in a lake for us.

It's a big old world and it's ours to squander—or to enjoy. We never had to earn our right to life or prove our value. That 200-year-old spell has now lifted. The cage door is finally open. Be brave.

Now put your shoes on and go outside.
