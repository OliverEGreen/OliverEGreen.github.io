I'm about to tell you a lot of things you'd rather not hear. I know this, because it's a nearly-universally uncomfortable truth. I feel a sense of dread as I'm writing. The ultimate elephant in the room. And, at some level, many of you already know everything I'm about to say.  

We are hurtling—actually, accelerating at lightspeed—towards a world that was only recently unthinkable; a world from a science-fiction novel. Which novel? We can't know yet. But, good or bad, it's going to be one of them.

I'd like to take this moment at the start of October to attempt to document where I see things moving in the next couple of years. The Overton Window is truly gliding, and much of this may come to seem obvious and mundane by the time next October rolls around. 

One nuance that's changed is that I no longer hope that I'm wrong. There's no way we're ever putting this genie back into its bottle. By now, that much should be obvious. Instead, we're on a rollercoaster, and all we can do is try to enjoy the ride and keep our arms and legs within the moving vehicle.

My best guesses as to what's coming. 

# The end of organisations

## Organisations bite

Organisations run by humans are *profoundly inefficient* things; case in point, just look at charities and how much of the money they receive goes towards their raison d'être. 

Our organisations suffer from internal politics, inertia, ideas of legacy and ego and who's really in charge. Much of their time, money and labour goes into running an organisation itself. Annual reviews, performative HR minefields, legalistic procedural bloat and nepotism. To a large degree, garden-variety corruption.

And in recent years, with movements encouraging people to "bring their whole selves" to work, they've been getting even worse. Just a glance at the staggering inefficiencies of projects like HS2 in the UK, whose [bat tunnel and its supporting works cost £216 million](https://www.bdonline.co.uk/news/full-cost-of-hs2-work-around-moxons-bat-tunnel-will-be-216m-says-dft/5136801.article). 

Simultaneously, we're witnessing the rapid and systematic hollowing-out of any value from our organisations. It used to be that we were some of the very few who could handle our specialist areas of expertise; it took years to graduate and learn the ropes, whether that was in solar farm permitting, software development, illustration or genetic therapy design. 

And then we let the AI in. Our productivity tool was also our Trojan Horse. And in a marketplace economy, using AI also became the necessary cost of staying competitive within our field. But, clearly, the AIs have been quietly absorbing all of our knowledge, our tricks and our idiosyncrasies.

Accountants are cooked. Even designers are being replaced by [highly skilled, nuanced and tasteful design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dyEpDobnkmA). It's only going to get better. It turns out the AI can do literally everything. 

Organisations often bring out a dark side to people. There's a saying "[science advances one funeral at a time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck%27s_principle)". The pride, hubris, ego, petty workplace politics, scrambling over resources. It gets worse as it scales; the larger an organisation becomes, the more places there are to hide our corruption, the more opportunities there are to hire your useless niece/nephew into a well-paid role. 

As time has shown, many of our most worthy organisations and ideas are first tainted, then corrupted and fully destroyed by our human fallibilities. It's time to hand these over to an objective, fair and well-oiled machine, lest we never escape our eternally-flawed human nature.

## Wear your Burger King crown with pride

And so, sticking to our old patterns, we all seize upon this once-in-a-lifetime opportunity to become the CEO of our own business empires. Like the mass-produced paper crowns Burger King used to give away, you may now anoint yourself CEO. Call your parents to let them know. Congratulations—you've made it.

Many apps now—[Grok Bots](https://www.reworked.co/collaboration-productivity/xai-launches-grok-bot-ai-agents-in-beta/), [Dots](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/29/openai-launches-dots-its-bubbly-agentic-avatar/)—let you run an entire one-person enterprise. The first one-man company valued at over a billion dollars? [That's already happened, actually](https://linas.substack.com/p/firstonepersonunicorn). 

But just one thing. It's just a bit too easy, isn't it?

Cue a gigaflood of noise now entering the market. GitHub has already been falling apart with upload volumes this year, as everyone desperately tries to launch their own visionary app, to grab attention with automated marketing, market research, outreach bots. "Please, whatever it takes, just help me escape the permanent underclass. Make no mistakes." 

They'll soon find that there's just not enough traction or demand in this world to keep their fledgling commercial business afloat. Yes, you can absolutely build it, they just won't come. And if it's really valuable, they'll point an LLM at it and build their own. Unfortunately, that's already the reality we find ourselves in.

Within the next few years, we'll begin to see the idea of human-run organisations just not making that much sense anymore. 

## Refactoring everything

A side effect of our current predicament is that gradually, and then rapidly, the systems we used to run our lives will begin to redesign themselves and become much faster, more fair, *much* more efficient.

I used to think that AI would meaningfully start running the world when it became obviously better than humans at all (or at least *most*) tasks. Once upon a time they called this 'AGI', and we're not quite there yet.

But watching how it's already impacting the world, I now think the days of AI running the show will arrive a little sooner, perhaps ahead of AGI.

Here's a few examples of why:

* In London, it now takes around five years to argue your case before an employment tribunal. Did you know it now takes around five years to see an employment tribunal in London? Why the sudden jump? Of course, AI has been used to flood the court system with every possible minor grievance.
* The job search is fundamentally broken. Hidden 1pt white text. Prompt injection attempts. [Jack and Jill](https://www.jackandjill.ai/) where it's AI vs AI. 
* Online dating. Famously another marketplace, of sorts. Also showing signs of degradation. Some users using AI to game the system. Sending people heading back offline into the real world. 

These are major tensions, surfacing today, which AI has either created or accelerated. To the point where the only solution remaining is to use even more AI, i.e. *to fight fire with fire.* 

For example, my own remortgage took 2 and a half years to complete. There were never any issues, it should have been completed in milliseconds. But the Land Registry was suffering a large backlog of cases due to Covid-19, and so *officially* my apartment I'd been living in for years didn't exist yet. There are many such cases.

And the AI insists upon itself; as it redesigns some of these systems, incredible pressures will begin to mount on others. Already, the wait for an employment tribunal hearing in London has skyrocketed from months to 5 years at the time of writing.

The LLMs' inexhaustible supply of knowledge labour has met some very high historically pent-up demand. So the pressure has moved further down the chain and it's now infeasible that this will ever be resolved without using more AI to fix the issue. Like so, everything will cascadingly collapse into full automation.

The next side effect I want to write about is going to be even stranger. I suspect it may be even harder to handle than the end of work.

# The end of money

## Spend your shells now

Many people have been bleating about Universal Basic Income for decades now. We've even run a few large-scale trials to see what happens. 

It's a nice idea, and I suspect that UBI probably will come into existence, at least for a short time. But, beyond this, I suspect something even stranger may begin to take place. The *idea of money* itself may begin to rot.

We need to remember that, despite how central it seems to everything in our lives, money is really just an idea that we've collectively subscribed to. It also has the unique quality that it's mathematically infinite. Because of this, amassing wealth becomes a game that humans have been playing since forever. Nobody can ever win, as there can always be a new wealthiest person.

Money is a powerful idea. A status indicator. It doesn't need to be notes or coins, it can be literally anything that we elect as a proxy for value. Only relatively recently, we [discovered tribes in the Highlands of Papua New Guinea](https://notnottalmud.substack.com/p/why-i-cant-stop-thinking-about-papua) which still traded shells.

The Australians figured this out and were able to supply the shells by the planeload in exchange for access to their natural resources. Soon, the shells became nearly worthless. What's scary is that we may genuinely find ourselves in their shoes some day soon. Our coins, our notes—our bank balances.

## How can this be?

Because Musk's oft-stated vision of hyperabundance may really come to pass. As our systems rapidly redesign and automate themselves, the cost of doing business *across the board* is going to fall.

We'll start to see a flood of automated competition appearing in any commercial areas that are still uncompetitive, depressing prices to near-unthinkable lows. 

So why use money anymore? What you need will be available to you for very low cost or for free. In an age of hyperabundance, money stops having much use, as it was only ever a useful proxy for something of value to us. A means to an end, not an end in itself.

We may no longer need such a proxy.

Like the Zimbabwean dollar, if you're a legacy wealthy holdout and still cling to the idea of money, you may be able to command extreme volumes of wealth. But, paradoxically, it will begin to account for very little once the society you operate within ceases to care about money all that much. It thus becomes an antiquated idea. Insisting upon the importance of financial wealth might just make you sound like a bewildered, outmoded crank. 

What a wild plot twist that might be. It might be time to put your wealth to work while it can still achieve something lasting.

## What remains

Removing money does not delete the idea of value. Just because knowledge work is essentially free, it doesn't mean that everything is now worthless. 

Indeed, money could still be incredibly useful for many cases where robotics and knowledge automation still cannot solve an underlying demand vs supply problem. Those cases where things still retain their innate value, or supply is inherently limited: 

* Rare materials and mineral rights. Perhaps land ownership, too, although I can see that becoming nationalised.
* Rare, high-value genetics. Hot people. At least until the sexbots and commercial genetic editing arrive. Then we're in that awful Bruce Willis movie, [*Surrogates*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrogates), I guess.
* Social skills. Embedding in a community. Being likeable. If anything, the value of this will probably rise.
